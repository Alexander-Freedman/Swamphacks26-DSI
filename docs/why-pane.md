# Why-pane copy
The why-pane is the small box beside each learning screen: what you are doing, why it works, and one source. Copy lives in `web/src/learn/why.json`.
Three lines per entry: `doing` (max 12 words, second person, present tense), `why` (max 25 words, one plain sentence on the mechanism), `source` (author, year, 3 to 6 words on the finding).
Rule: copy stays under those word limits, no jargon, hedging, emojis or em-dashes, and only sources found in the Mental Models Tool research files. Unsure? Cite "Onionskin research brief, section N".

| Stage | Doing | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| guess | Write your best guess before you see anything. | A guess you committed to, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick harder when it arrives. | Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009, wrong guesses boost later learning |
| anchor | Use one true fact to predict what happens next. | A real fact gives you something solid to reason from, and a prediction shows you where your picture is thin. | Onionskin research brief, section 9: start concrete, then abstract |
| contrast | Compare two look-alike cases and write why they differ. | Lining up two cases makes your mind match what each part does, so the shared structure shows up on its own. | Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003, comparing cases beats studying separately |
| stress | Test your fix on three extreme cases and watch what breaks. | Extreme cases expose rules that only work in easy cases, so a break shows exactly which part of your model is wrong. | Clement 2009, experts test models with extreme cases |
| draw | Sketch the model from memory, then write its rule. | Drawing makes you pick the parts, link them, and put them together, and a wrong picture shows a wrong idea. | Wammes, Meade & Fernandes 2016, drawing roughly doubles recall |
| neighbor | Pick which idea this is, against its look-alike cousin. | Telling similar ideas apart is a skill of its own, and practicing the choice teaches you when each one applies. | Brunmair & Richter 2019, mixing look-alikes sharpens discrimination |
| transfer | Say the same idea in a totally different field. | Ideas rarely carry over to new settings on their own, so you build the bridge by naming where else it applies. | Barnett & Ceci 2002, far transfer must be designed for |
| done | Read the mistake card, then save it to your Notebook. | Seeing why a wrong idea felt right lets you replace the idea itself, not just patch one answer. | Chi 2005, stubborn misconceptions need a new category |
